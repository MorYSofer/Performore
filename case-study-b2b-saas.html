The product truth did the converting. The persona only decided who leaned in.

Four things changed.

In roughly this order, because each one only pays off once the one before it is in place.

01 · Weeks 1–4

Fix the signals before touching the ads

Optimising creative against a broken conversion event just teaches the algorithm faster in the wrong direction. So the tracking went first.

  • Server-side conversions to both platforms, deduplicated against the browser event
  • One registration event split into three: account created, first workflow completed, first payment
  • Values attached to each, so bidding chases the customers who stay
  • Enriched match data, lifting Meta's event quality out of the range where targeting guesses
3 Optimisation events, valued, where there had been one
02 · Weeks 3–8

Find the product truth, then cast it

We went looking for the specific thing the product removes from someone's week. One sentence, from support tickets and cancellation calls rather than the pitch deck. That sentence became the spine of every ad.

  • The truth held constant; the person telling it changed
  • Distinct buyer personas — the founder, the ops lead who inherits the process, the person who has to sign off the spend
  • Each one got the same promise phrased in the language of its own bad afternoon
  • Winning angles rebuilt in four formats rather than retired at the first sign of fatigue
1 Product truth, told through several buyer personas
03 · Ongoing, weekly

Testing and production as one loop

Testing without a production line stalls the moment a winner appears and there is nothing to follow it with. We ran them as a single weekly cadence so the queue never emptied.

  • One variable per test — hook, or format, or persona, never all three
  • Results read on hook rate and cost per qualified customer, not on likes
  • Losers cut on a fixed threshold, so no ad survived on sentiment
  • Winners immediately iterated into the next week's batch
Weekly Test, read, cut, rebuild — for eleven months
04 · From week 6

Build the funnel a decision actually takes

Nobody changes the software their team works in on a first impression. The retargeting was rebuilt in layers, so the second, third and fourth touch each had a job instead of repeating the first.

  • Audiences split by depth of engagement, not just by recency window
  • A different objection answered at each layer
  • Frequency capped per layer so the ladder persuades instead of nagging
  • Budget weighted toward the layers that were actually closing
4 Retargeting layers, each with its own message

The four touches.

Most of the customers in this account did not convert on the ad that introduced them. They converted on the second, third or fourth — which is only possible if those touches say something new.

01 Cold · prospecting

Name the problem

The product truth, delivered in the voice of one specific buyer persona. No feature list, no pricing. The only job is recognition.

“You did this manually again last week, didn't you.”
02 Engaged · 3–7 days

Show the mechanism

They recognised themselves and left. Now the ad proves the thing is real: the actual screen, the actual number of minutes it takes.

“Here is the whole thing, in about four minutes.”
03 Visited · 7–14 days

Answer the objection

By now the hesitation is specific — switching cost, migration, whether the rest of the team will actually adopt it. So the ad addresses that, not the original promise.

“Moving your data across takes an afternoon. Here's how.”
04 Abandoned · 14–30 days

Remove the last reason to wait

They started and stopped. Proof from a business that looks like theirs, and the smallest possible next step — finish the half-built account, nothing more.

“Your workspace is still there. Two fields left.”

The reason this lowers acquisition cost rather than just adding spend: layers two through four talk to people the first ad already paid for. Every customer recovered there arrives against a click that was bought once.
